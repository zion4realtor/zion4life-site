At the end of October, pumpkins, spooky decorations, and children in costumes appear around many neighborhoods. Some Christian families join community activities; others choose not to celebrate. Each year, people ask: **Why do some Christians avoid Halloween?**

The Bible does not mention the modern holiday by name, nor does it issue a blanket rule about every activity held on October 31. Christians often make this decision by considering what a particular activity involves, their conscience before God, and whether participating will help them and their families draw near to the Lord. We can take spiritual boundaries seriously without replacing truth with fear or condemnation.

## Understand the history—and the activities today

The name Halloween is connected with the evening before All Saints’ Day. Accounts of its development often discuss the ancient Celtic festival of Samhain, later Christian observances, and folk customs that took shape in different places. Costumes, jack-o’-lanterns, neighborhood visits for treats, and commercial celebrations have also changed over generations and across cultures. The history cannot be reduced to a single religious practice, and a tradition’s past alone does not determine what every person means by taking part today. 

So we need not label every neighbor or parent taking children trick-or-treating as worshiping spirits. At the same time, commercialization or entertainment does not erase the fact that some activities may involve divination, spirit communication, seeking guidance from the spirit world, or making evil seem attractive. Christians should look honestly at what they themselves are being invited to do.

## The boundaries Scripture gives us

Deuteronomy 18:9–14 forbids God’s people from seeking guidance through divination, sorcery, mediums, or communication with the dead. These verses are not discussing pumpkins, candy, or every costume. They do make clear that God’s people should not turn to occult practices or spiritual powers apart from God for guidance.

Ephesians 5 calls believers to discern what pleases the Lord and not to take part in the unfruitful works of darkness. If an activity invites us to treat spirits, witchcraft, communication with the dead, or evil as appealing or worthy of imitation, Christians have good reason to decline. We belong to Christ and are called to walk in the light.

That does not mean every neighbor with a spooky decoration or every parent accompanying a child has the same belief or intention. We can refuse activities we believe are inconsistent with Scripture while speaking carefully and refusing to condemn people lightly.

## Three questions to consider prayerfully

### Could this draw my heart away from the Lord?

When an activity involves divination, communicating with spirits, summoning the dead, or seeking answers from the spirit world, Scripture gives a clear boundary. We do not need to violate our faith because someone says, “It’s only for fun.” If your conscience tells you not to participate, you can say no calmly.

### Is it beneficial and does it build others up?

In 1 Corinthians 10, Paul reminds believers that not everything permissible is beneficial or constructive. Whatever we do should glorify God and consider the good of others. We can ask: What kind of imagination and values is this activity nurturing in my children? Does it help me love God and people, or make darkness and fear seem harmless?

### Can I respect another person’s conscience and show love?

Romans 14 teaches believers not to despise or judge one another over certain personal convictions. Each person should be settled before the Lord. If you choose not to celebrate, you do not need to persuade others through fear or shame. And if another Christian, with a clear conscience, joins a community event that does not involve occult practices, we should not rush to label them. Paul also reminds us that Christian freedom is governed by love and must not become a stumbling block to others.

## Abstaining can still be a blessing

The heart of abstaining from Halloween is not fear of a date on the calendar. It is a desire to worship God, to consider what shapes our lives, and to love our neighbors well. Christians believe that Jesus Christ has overcome sin and death. Our hope is not in darkness, and we do not need to use fear to share the gospel.

If your family decides not to participate, explain your reasons gently to your children. You might invite neighbors for a meal, share stories from your life, or host an autumn gathering centered on the hope of the Bible. When you meet people at community events, you can still be kind and respectful; different choices do not have to become arguments. May people see the light of Christ in our words and lives.

Before deciding, take a quiet moment to pray: **Lord, guard my thoughts and conscience so that I honor You in my choices. Give me a heart that loves others and helps me bear witness to You with gentleness, truth, and hope. Amen.**
